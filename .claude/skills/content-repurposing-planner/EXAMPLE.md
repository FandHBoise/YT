# Content Repurposing Planner — Worked Examples

These two examples show what a real repurposing plan looks like when you run this skill. The first is a YouTube video, the second is a podcast episode. Both show full creator input and full generated output.

---

## Example 1: YouTube Video on Freelance Pricing

### Creator Input

> I just posted a 28-minute YouTube video called "Why I Stopped Charging Hourly (And What I Do Instead)." I'm a freelance video editor. The video walks through my real pricing history — I started at $25/hr, then went to $50/hr, kept running into scope creep, and eventually switched to project-based pricing. I show the actual invoice structures I use now and explain how I handle client pushback on pricing. My audience is other freelancers and aspiring video editors. I'm most active on YouTube, LinkedIn, and I send a weekly newsletter. About 40k YouTube subscribers.

---

### Generated Output

## Repurposing Plan: "Why I Stopped Charging Hourly (And What I Do Instead)"

**Source**: YouTube video (28 min)
**Core thesis**: Project-based pricing protects your time and income better than hourly billing — here's how to make the switch.
**Best audience**: Freelance video editors and creative freelancers earning $25-100/hr

---

### Content Pieces

**1. Short-form video clip — YouTube Shorts / Instagram Reels / TikTok**
- Angle: "The moment a client asked me to 'just add one more thing' for the 6th time" — use the scope creep story as a relatable hook
- Source moment: The section where you describe the specific job that broke your hourly model
- Brief: Cut a 45-60 second vertical clip showing just the story of the worst scope creep project. No graphics needed — straight to camera works. End with "That's when I realized hourly was never going to protect me." Leave the solution for the full video (drives clicks).
- Effort: Quick

**2. LinkedIn post — LinkedIn**
- Angle: The real reason freelancers undercharge (it's not confidence, it's structure)
- Source moment: Your pricing history from $25 to $50/hr and why the number wasn't the problem
- Brief: Write a 200-300 word post that opens with "I raised my rates twice and still felt broke." Walk through the insight that changing the pricing model matters more than changing the number. End with a question: "Are you pricing by time or by outcome?" Strong engagement format for the freelance and business audience on LinkedIn.
- Effort: Quick

**3. Newsletter section — Email list**
- Angle: "Here's the invoice structure I use now" — give them the thing
- Source moment: The section where you show your actual project-based invoice breakdown
- Brief: Write a 400-500 word newsletter section (not a full standalone email) that shares your project pricing template in plain text. Include the line items you use, how you structure revision rounds, and the one phrase you use when clients push back on price. Make it genuinely useful — this is the section readers screenshot and save.
- Effort: Medium

**4. Long-form blog post — Website / Medium**
- Angle: How to switch from hourly to project-based pricing (without losing clients)
- Source moment: Full video — use the whole arc
- Brief: Write a 1,200-1,500 word post structured as a practical guide. Use your personal story as the opening hook, then walk through the actual steps: how to audit your current rates, how to structure a project-based proposal, and how to handle the first client who says "your old rate was cheaper." Include the invoice structure from the video as a scannable section. This post can rank for "project-based pricing for freelancers" and should link back to the YouTube video.
- Effort: Deep

**5. Twitter/X thread — X (Twitter)**
- Angle: "I made $50/hr and still felt broke. Here's why hourly pricing is broken for freelancers" — 8-tweet thread
- Source moment: The core argument of the video
- Brief: Open with the attention-grabbing hook above. Tweet 2: the scope creep problem. Tweets 3-5: how project pricing works and what changed. Tweet 6: the one objection you always hear. Tweet 7: what to say to that client. Tweet 8: the result (time, income, sanity). Link to full video in the last tweet. Keep each tweet under 250 characters with punchy line breaks — no full paragraphs.
- Effort: Quick

**6. Carousel post — LinkedIn / Instagram**
- Angle: "5 signs you're ready to switch from hourly to project pricing"
- Source moment: The implicit checklist implied throughout the video
- Brief: 6-slide carousel. Slide 1: hook. Slides 2-6: one sign per slide (e.g., "You've had a project double in scope with no extra pay," "Your hourly rate punishes you for getting faster"). Each slide is one sentence + short explanation. Final slide: CTA to watch the full video. Dark background, clean typography — matches your brand.
- Effort: Medium

**7. Quote graphic — Instagram / LinkedIn**
- Angle: Pull the most shareable line from the video
- Source moment: Whichever moment you said something that made you think "I should clip that"
- Brief: Design a simple graphic with the quote. If you do not have a design tool, Canva works fine. Keep it clean — the quote should do the work, not the graphic. Suggested lines to pull: "Charging by the hour means your income goes down as you get better." or "Scope creep is not a client problem. It's a contract problem."
- Effort: Quick

---

### Start Here (Top 3 Picks)
1. **Short-form clip (Reels/Shorts/TikTok)** — The scope creep story is a universal pain point that stops the scroll cold. Easiest to cut, highest reach potential.
2. **LinkedIn post** — Your audience skews toward the freelance/business crowd that lives on LinkedIn. This angle ("it's not your rate, it's your model") is built for that platform.
3. **Newsletter section with the invoice structure** — Giving your actual template is rare and valuable. This is the piece your subscribers will forward to other freelancers.

---

### Skip or Deprioritize
- **Podcast clip**: Unless you have a podcast already, creating an audio-only clip from a video essay does not add much. Your strong suit here is visual — stay there.
- **Full blog post**: Worth doing eventually, but it's the highest effort piece. Do the quick wins first so you have momentum before sitting down for a deep writing session.

---

### Why This Works

The video has three ingredients that make repurposing easy: a strong personal story (the scope creep moment), a concrete system (the invoice structure), and a clear before/after arc. That combination gives you emotional hooks for social, practical depth for newsletters, and a tutorial structure for long-form writing — three completely different content needs, all from one source.

The LinkedIn post and short-form clip do not compete with each other or with the full video. They each give a different slice of the argument, so a viewer who sees the clip and reads the LinkedIn post still has a reason to watch the full 28 minutes.

## Example 2: Podcast Episode on Burnout Recovery

### Creator Input

> I host a podcast for creators called "Making It Without Losing It." My latest episode is 52 minutes long — I interviewed a full-time YouTuber with 800k subscribers who burned out completely in 2023, took 4 months off, and came back with a completely different relationship to content creation. We talked about the warning signs she missed, what the break actually looked like (she did not post at all), how she told her audience, and the systems she built after returning that keep her from burning out again. My platforms: YouTube (podcast clips already), Instagram, newsletter. 8,000 podcast listeners.

---

### Generated Output

## Repurposing Plan: "Burnout, a 4-Month Break, and Coming Back Stronger" (Making It Without Losing It, Ep. XX)

**Source**: Podcast episode (52 min), interview format
**Core thesis**: Burnout is not a character flaw — it's a systems failure. Here's what recovery actually looked like for one creator, and how she rebuilt.
**Best audience**: Full-time and aspiring content creators dealing with sustainability and mental health around creative work

---

### Content Pieces

**1. Podcast clip — YouTube / Instagram Reels**
- Angle: "The warning signs I completely ignored" — let the guest tell it in her own words
- Source moment: The segment where she lists the signals she dismissed before the crash
- Brief: Pull a 60-90 second clip of the guest describing her warning signs. This works cold — no intro needed beyond a text overlay with her name and subscriber count. The specificity ("I started dreading editing, then I started dreading filming, then I stopped opening my camera bag") is what makes it land. Post on YouTube as a clip and repost to Reels. Add captions.
- Effort: Quick

**2. Podcast clip — YouTube / Instagram Reels**
- Angle: "I had 800k subscribers and I just... stopped posting. Here's what happened."
- Source moment: The moment she describes telling her audience she was taking a break
- Brief: Second clip focused on the actual break announcement — what she said, how her audience responded, and what surprised her about the reaction. This is the high-drama moment in the episode and it is genuinely counterintuitive (most creators fear that going quiet will tank their channel). Keep it under 90 seconds. Strong hook for creators who are afraid to take breaks.
- Effort: Quick

**3. Instagram carousel — Instagram**
- Angle: "5 warning signs you're heading for creator burnout (that I ignored for 6 months)"
- Source moment: The warning signs section of the interview
- Brief: Distill the guest's warning signs into a 6-slide carousel in your voice as the host. Slide 1: "She had 800k subscribers and still burned out. Here are the signs she missed." Slides 2-6: one warning sign per slide with a one-sentence explanation. Final slide: "Full story on the podcast — link in bio." Do not make this a generic burnout checklist. Ground each point in what the guest actually experienced.
- Effort: Medium

**4. Newsletter — Email list**
- Angle: What taking 4 months off actually looked like — and what she rebuilt when she came back
- Source moment: The "what did the break actually look like" and "systems after returning" sections
- Brief: Write a 500-600 word email in your voice as host. This is not a podcast recap — it's a reflection. Open with why this conversation stayed with you. Then share 2-3 specific things the guest said that you had not heard before (the actual mechanics of the break, how she structured her return, what the new systems look like). End with a question for your readers: "Have you ever taken a real break? What happened?" This format drives replies and builds community.
- Effort: Medium

**5. Twitter/X thread — X (Twitter)**
- Angle: "We talked to a creator with 800k subscribers who walked away for 4 months. Here's what she learned." — 7-tweet thread
- Source moment: Full episode arc
- Brief: Tweet 1: hook (the stats + what she did). Tweets 2-4: warning signs, what the break looked like, and what surprised her about her audience's reaction. Tweets 5-6: the two or three systems she built after returning. Tweet 7: link to full episode. Write in the guest's perspective ("She told me...") — you're the narrator, not the expert here. Keep each tweet punchy, no threads that feel like paragraphs.
- Effort: Quick

**6. Standalone short video — YouTube Shorts / Instagram Reels**
- Angle: You (the host) sharing the one thing from the interview that changed how you think about your own content schedule
- Source moment: Whatever the guest said that genuinely surprised or shifted you
- Brief: Film a 45-60 second to-camera video in your own words. You are not recapping the episode — you are sharing a personal reaction. "I interviewed someone this week who said something I've been thinking about for days..." This format is more personal than a clip and shows your own perspective, which deepens the host-audience relationship. Direct people to the episode at the end.
- Effort: Quick

---

### Start Here (Top 3 Picks)
1. **Warning signs clip** — It's the most universally relatable moment in the episode and requires zero production work beyond trimming and adding captions. Post it the day the episode drops.
2. **Newsletter** — Your 8,000 listeners are an engaged group. A thoughtful email about this episode with your personal take will drive listen-throughs and replies. Write it in 45 minutes while the conversation is still fresh.
3. **Instagram carousel (warning signs)** — The list format is made for Instagram saves, which feeds the algorithm. This is the piece most likely to reach creators who are not yet in your audience.

---

### Skip or Deprioritize
- **Blog post**: The episode has strong emotional and narrative content, but turning a 52-minute interview into a blog post that does it justice takes significant time. The clips, newsletter, and carousel will drive more podcast growth for less effort. Revisit if you want an SEO play later.
- **LinkedIn**: This episode and its themes could work on LinkedIn, but your audience skews toward a different creator profile there. Unless your LinkedIn following is already engaged with creator health topics, deprioritize for now.

---

### Why This Works

Interview episodes are among the easiest to repurpose because the guest's voice does the heavy lifting. You do not need to paraphrase or write around the content — you pull direct quotes and clips, which are more credible and compelling than anything you could write secondhand.

This episode also has a structural advantage: it has a clear before/after arc (burnout → break → return), which gives every piece a natural narrative shape. The warning signs section is built for lists. The break announcement is built for a clip. The post-burnout systems are built for a newsletter. Each format gets the part of the story it is best suited to carry.

The host-perspective Reel (piece 6) is easy to overlook but worth doing — it reminds your audience that there is a real person behind the show and not just a content delivery mechanism.

---

## Key Takeaways Across Both Examples

**Specificity is the whole game.** Both plans work because they are built around specific moments, specific quotes, and specific insights — not "make social content from your episode." The more specific your source material, the more specific and useful the repurposing plan.

**Not all formats fit all content.** The freelance pricing video is heavy on systems and structure, which makes it great for long-form writing. The burnout episode is heavy on story and emotion, which makes it great for clips and personal newsletters. The skill matches format to content type, not the other way around.

**Three quick wins beat ten mediocre pieces.** In both plans, the "Start here" picks are all achievable in under two hours and do not require a production team. Start with those before committing to the heavier pieces.

**Repurposing is not recycling.** Each piece takes a different angle on the source material. The LinkedIn post does not summarize the YouTube video — it makes a different argument using the same underlying insight. The newsletter does not recap the podcast — it offers the host's personal reaction. Repurposing done right gives each platform something worth stopping for.
